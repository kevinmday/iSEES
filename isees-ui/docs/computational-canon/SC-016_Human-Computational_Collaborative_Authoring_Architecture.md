\# SC-016 — Human-Computational Collaborative Authoring Architecture



\*\*Status:\*\* Canonical

\*\*Classification:\*\* Foundational Computational Architecture

\*\*Depends On:\*\* SC-013 Computational Observation Architecture, SC-014 Computational Authoring and Narrative Architecture, SC-015 Computational Publication and Knowledge Dissemination Architecture



\---



\# 1. Purpose



This canon establishes the architecture governing collaboration between human researchers and computational instruments during the construction of canonical investigative knowledge.



Within iSEES, authoring is not performed exclusively by either humans or computational systems.



Instead, canonical knowledge emerges through an iterative collaboration in which humans contribute judgment, intent, experience, and interpretation while computational instruments contribute organization, reasoning, analysis, generation, verification, and transformation.



The computational document remains the authoritative representation throughout this collaboration.



\---



\# 2. Fundamental Principle



Humans remain the authors.



Computational instruments remain collaborators.



No computational instrument possesses authority over the canonical document.



All computational modifications require explicit human acceptance unless otherwise authorized by the researcher.



The author always retains final editorial responsibility.



\---



\# 3. Human-Computational Collaboration



Collaboration is an intentional dialogue between the researcher and computational instruments.



Neither participant independently produces the completed investigative work.



Instead, knowledge evolves through repeated cycles of:



\* observation

\* organization

\* computation

\* interpretation

\* revision

\* validation

\* refinement



The canonical document continuously reflects this collaborative process.



\---



\# 4. Iterative Authoring



Computational authoring is inherently iterative.



A researcher may:



\* collect observations

\* organize computational objects

\* request narrative generation

\* modify generated language

\* request computational refinement

\* expand supporting evidence

\* reorganize investigative structure

\* regenerate selected narratives

\* continue manual editing



The document therefore evolves through repeated interaction rather than sequential production.



\---



\# 5. Mixed Authorship



Canonical documents may simultaneously contain:



\* entirely human-authored narrative

\* computationally generated narrative

\* human revisions of generated narrative

\* computational refinements of human narrative

\* collaboratively authored sections

\* structured computational objects awaiting projection



No distinction is made regarding the validity of knowledge based solely upon authorship.



Knowledge derives its authority from evidence rather than origin.



\---



\# 6. Computational Narrative Objects



Computational instruments should preferentially operate upon structured narrative objects rather than free-form text.



These structures may contain:



\* observations

\* graph relationships

\* artifacts

\* hypotheses

\* timelines

\* claims

\* supporting evidence

\* contradictory evidence

\* intended audience

\* narrative objectives

\* projection preferences



Natural language becomes one possible realization of these computational structures.



\---



\# 7. Human Editing



Human editing never invalidates computational relationships.



Researchers remain free to:



\* rewrite paragraphs

\* replace sentences

\* expand explanations

\* remove generated language

\* introduce entirely new prose



while preserving computational linkage to the originating observations whenever appropriate.



Computational identity therefore survives editorial refinement.



\---



\# 8. Computational Instruments



Studio supports specialized computational instruments rather than a monolithic artificial intelligence.



Examples include:



\* narrative generation

\* contradiction analysis

\* citation assistance

\* evidence verification

\* investigative expansion

\* hypothesis generation

\* timeline construction

\* style transformation

\* translation

\* executive summarization

\* computational review

\* publication preparation



Each instrument performs a narrowly defined computational responsibility.



Together they form a collaborative authoring environment.



\---



\# 9. Structured Collaboration



Whenever practical, computational instruments should exchange structured computational objects instead of natural language.



Structured representations preserve:



\* semantic intent

\* provenance

\* observation references

\* investigative relationships

\* computational reasoning

\* reproducibility



Narrative language is generated only when appropriate for human communication.



\---



\# 10. Human Judgment



Certain responsibilities remain exclusively human.



These include:



\* investigative direction

\* interpretation

\* ethical judgment

\* publication decisions

\* acceptance of computational suggestions

\* final editorial approval



Computational instruments assist these activities but do not replace them.



\---



\# 11. Computational Sessions



Collaborative authoring occurs within a Computational Authoring Session.



The session records computational interactions including:



\* selected observations

\* invoked computational instruments

\* generated computational structures

\* accepted recommendations

\* rejected recommendations

\* regeneration history

\* narrative revisions



The session documents the evolution of the investigative process without replacing the canonical document itself.



\---



\# 12. Regeneration



Narrative regeneration is non-destructive.



Researchers may request regeneration when:



\* new observations become available

\* additional evidence is discovered

\* narrative style changes

\* publication audience changes

\* computational instruments improve



Human-authored revisions remain protected unless the researcher explicitly requests replacement.



\---



\# 13. Computational Review



Collaborative authoring enables continuous computational review.



Instruments may identify:



\* unsupported assertions

\* missing evidence

\* contradictory observations

\* unresolved hypotheses

\* incomplete timelines

\* weak investigative coverage

\* citation deficiencies

\* narrative inconsistencies



These findings become recommendations rather than automatic modifications.



\---



\# 14. Instrument Independence



No computational instrument owns the document.



Each instrument contributes independently through well-defined computational interfaces.



Future reasoning systems, language models, deterministic algorithms, symbolic systems, or hybrid computational methods may replace individual instruments without altering the architecture established by this canon.



The collaborative framework therefore remains technology-independent.



\---



\# 15. Relationship to Computational Authoring



SC-014 establishes that computational observations become authored knowledge.



This canon defines how humans and computational instruments cooperatively construct that knowledge through iterative collaboration.



\---



\# 16. Relationship to Publication



SC-015 governs dissemination of canonical knowledge.



This canon governs the collaborative process that precedes publication.



Publication communicates completed knowledge.



Collaboration constructs completed knowledge.



\---



\# 17. Canonical Workflow



The collaborative lifecycle becomes:



Computational Observation



↓



Research Collection



↓



Authorial Organization



↓



Computational Assistance



↓



Human Revision



↓



Computational Analysis



↓



Human Validation



↓



Canonical Document



↓



Publication



The document continuously evolves through intentional collaboration between researcher and computational instruments.



\---



\# 18. Foundational Principle



The objective of collaborative authoring is not to automate research.



Its objective is to augment human reasoning while preserving human judgment, computational provenance, investigative transparency, and authorial control.



Within iSEES, computational instruments function as collaborators.



The researcher remains the investigator.



The computational document remains the canonical expression of knowledge produced through their collaboration.
